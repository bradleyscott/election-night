The electorate column · four ways to split the card from the table

proposal

A seat page shows the same candidates twice, in the same order of importance, and neither panel says which question it answers. The Win % card lists a party, a name and a vote count for the top two, sorts them by chance, and carries the margin in small type. The table underneath lists every candidate with a party and a vote count, sorts them by votes, and ends in a % column that never says what it is a share of. Two surfaces, two orderings, two unlabelled vocabularies — the reader has to work out that one is a forecast and the other is a count.

It gets worse in the one case the model exists for. When the votes counted favour one candidate and the projection favours the other, the card lists the trailing candidate first, with the leading candidate's votes printed beneath them, and the table directly below reverses the order. Nothing on either panel says why, so the page looks broken exactly when it is doing its job. Every design below is tried on both configurations: the ordinary one, where the two agree, and the hard one, where they do not.

The constraint every design keeps The chance is a projection onto everything still to be counted — tonight's outstanding votes and the special vote — so it is not a second opinion about the count, it is a statement about a different question. That is the fact the page has to make legible. A design that hides it (by dropping the number) or blurs it (by printing both facts under one heading) fails even if it removes the duplication. Also unchanged: a finished count prints the margin instead of a percentage, party view drops the projection entirely, and before the first result the panel says when results are expected.

Where the duplication is
  • 1Party, name and vote count appear in both panels — 8,412 and 8,019 are each printed twice, 40px apart.
  • 2Neither panel has a title, so nothing says one is a forecast and the other is a count. Win % is a label, not a subject.
  • 3The table’s % column is a share of votes counted. Read beside the card’s percentages it looks like the same number on a different scale, and 42.4 next to 54 invites a subtraction.
  • 4The two panels sort by different keys and never say so.
  • 5The margin runs twice, as a Ahead by line on the card and implicitly between the two vote figures in the table — and on the card it is attached to the chance ordering, so it reads as a contradiction in the hard case.
  • 6Nothing says the chance includes the special vote, counted a fortnight later. That is the main reason a candidate can trail on the night and still be favoured, and the page never mentions it.

Costnothing here is wrong. Every number is true and the model is sound. The panel simply never states its two questions, so the reader has to infer them from the column headers — and one of those headers is a bare %.

What it fixes
  • 1The card prints no vote count. It names the likely winner, prints one number, and draws one bar split across the hundred — the whole contest in one object instead of two rows of small print.
  • 2Both panels are titled with the question they answer, so the duplication stops being a coincidence and becomes a division of labour.
  • 3% becomes Vote % — the label used in every design in this file. It pairs with Win % beside it, so the two percentages cannot be mistaken for each other, and the caption states the denominator again where the number sits.
  • 4The table’s caption says what its ordering is, which is the only ordering a count can honestly have.
  • 5The margin leaves the card. It is the count’s fact, and the count is the table.
  • 6In the hard case only, the card gains one sentence naming who leads the count and why the projection still favours someone else — see the panel below. In the ordinary case that sentence would be noise, so it is conditional.

Costthe runner-up loses her vote count on the card, so the reader who wants “how close is it” has to look down a panel. And the two panels still sit in different orders in the hard case — this design removes the duplication but not the disagreement; it only labels it.

  • 1There is nothing to duplicate. Each candidate appears once, with every fact the page has about them on one line.
  • 2There is nothing to contradict either: one ordering, so the hard case cannot produce two columns facing opposite ways. The chance sits beside the votes it disagrees with, which is the explanation.

What it fixes
  • 1The card carries a fact that appears nowhere else: the split of the outstanding vote that changes the answer. 47.9% is arithmetic — (9,300 − 393) ÷ (2 × 9,300) — not a model output, and it is the same fact as the probability, said in the currency of votes.
  • 2It is written as a sentence with a named candidate, so there is no row order to contradict anything. In the hard case the sentence simply names the other candidate, and the table never has to agree with an ordering it cannot see.
  • 3The probability survives as a small line at the foot — still present, no longer the loudest thing, which stops a mid-count number being read as a score.
  • 4% is named, and the table has a caption like A.

Costthe headline number of the whole page is now a derived percentage the reader must reason about, and it needs two sentences to set up. It also only works while the lead is small relative to the votes out: at 92% counted the same sentence demands 50.4%, which is true, dull, and no longer explains anything. The percentage has to come back for the settled case, which means the card changes shape twice on one night.

The seam rule is the kind of device that reads as clever the first time and as furniture the twentieth. And the two orderings still differ in the hard case — the design explains the difference rather than preventing it.

At 390px · B on a phone, before and after the fix

Below sm the detail column is the full width of the phone — 356px of content in a 390px viewport. B has five columns, and squeezed to that width the party column takes 115px while the candidate name gets 88px: the label is wider than the thing it labels, so names wrap to two lines, and the table is still 28px wider than its card, which clips the Win % column even after everything has been squeezed. Measured in the browser at 390px, not estimated.

The fix is structural: below sm the party stops being a column and becomes a second line under the candidate’s name, in space that line already occupies. Win % keeps its own right-hand column, so it is on screen without a swipe. The table folds a column into a cell exactly as the Flipped page’s mobile table already folds its numbers (majority 2,392 · 5.9%), and it is the same party field rendered twice for two layouts, not a second source.

How it is built
  • 1The Party header and every Party cell carry hidden sm:table-cell; the candidate cell carries the party again, sm:hidden. Two renders of one field, not two sources — and the column, its header and every cell in it disappear together.
  • 2Numeric cells get whitespace-nowrap and the type drops a step (text-sm sm:text-base), so the four remaining columns fit inside 328px without overflow.
  • 3The 56px Win % track is dropped below sm. At this width it was decoration anyway; the number is the reading.
  • 4No column widths are set. With the party folded the candidate cell is given 139px against the 88px it had, because three numeric columns that cannot wrap need only what they need — so a long name, a macron-heavy Māori seat name or a by-election surname wraps inside the cell instead of pushing the table into a scroll.
What it costs

The party repeats on every row where the desktop table states it once, and a folded row is 57px against A’s single-line 37px, so the table itself grows from 218 to 316px. That is not a reason to prefer A: B has no card above it, so at 390px the whole stack is still 494px against A’s 543px. The fold spends none of B’s height advantage — the naive render was 334px because every name wrapped, so folding the party makes the table shorter than the version it replaces as well as legible.

The pointB’s desktop case was never the weak one; its phone case was, and it was a column count rather than a layout problem. With the party folded the table fits 360px (326px of content, zero overflow) in four columns and the chance never leaves the first screen — so the remaining argument against B is the one the table cannot answer: there is no large number at the top of the page saying who wins.

  Where the chance lives Duplication The hard case Panel stack
Today card, twice over with votes party, name and votes on both panels two facing orderings, unexplained 708px
A card only party and name only a sentence on the card names the leader 666px
B a column of the table none — one row per candidate impossible: one ordering, both facts 491px

The choice is really one question: should the probability keep the top of the page? A and C say yes and pay for it by asking the reader to look at two panels; B says no and pays for it by making the answer a cell in a table; D says yes but spends a rule and a label making the handover explicit.

Recommendation: A. It is the smallest change to what ships — the card keeps its shape, loses its votes, and gains one sentence for the case that currently looks like a bug; the table keeps every row it has and gains a title, a caption and an honest column header. It removes every number that was printed twice without removing the glanceable verdict, and it does not ask the reader to learn a new object. C is the better card for a reader who wants the path rather than the verdict, and 47.9% is a genuinely new fact — but it needs two sentences to set up and stops being interesting at 92% counted, which means the card changes shape twice in one night. B should not be dismissed on the 512px evidence above, where its cost is invisible. Its phone case was the weak one, and it was a column count rather than a layout: folding the party under the name makes the table fit a 360px phone in four columns, no fixed widths and no sideways scroll, and leaves the stack 49px shorter than A’s. With that paid, the choice between A and B is one question and no longer a trade-off — whether the verdict is a card above the numbers or a column inside them.

Provenance Banks Peninsula, the two candidates and the shape of the 2023 result are real; the partial count, the three minor candidates and both chance figures are constructed to be self-consistent (the shares sum to the counted total, the two chances sum to 100, and 47.9% is (9,300 − 393) ÷ (2 × 9,300)). The tokens, utilities and panel classes are the real ones; the markup is hand-drawn and is not the component.
