Chance of winning · four ways to set it

proposal

One card, four designs. Every card in the first four sections holds the same seat at the same moment — Banks Peninsula, 68% counted, 54% for Vanessa Weenink — at the 512px the detail column gives it on a 1400px page. At the foot of the page each design is repeated on a seat that is nearly settled (92%), because a card that only works at 54 is not a card. Only the design moves.

It has to answer one question at a glance — who is likely to hold this seat — and a second on a second look — why. The number is the hard part: a percentage near the middle is easy to mistake for a score, and a percentage near the top is easy to mistake for a call. Everything else on the card is context for it: the count’s progress, the current margin, and the votes still out (9,300 of them, more than twice the 393 that separate the two). The tokens, utilities and component classes are the real ones; the markup is hand-drawn.

ConstraintOnce the count is complete the number would have to read 100%, which tells a reader nothing, so the card switches to the fact — winner, margin, and the special votes still to come. Party view renders nothing at all: a party contests the party vote in seats it nominates no candidate for. Neither changes below; what changes is how the counting state earns its space.

Why repeat themAt 92% three of the four say something they could not say at 54%: the bar is nearly one colour, the grid is nearly full, the marker is at the far end of its scale. A has to keep the losing colour legible at 7%, B has to keep 92 squares from reading as a finished count, and C has to say “clear of a coin toss” without looking like a call — which is the one place the printed number, not the drawing, has to carry the caution.
