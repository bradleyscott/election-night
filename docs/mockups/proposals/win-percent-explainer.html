How we work out Win %

explainer

Win % is our best estimate of who wins an electorate once every vote is counted: the votes counted tonight, the votes still to come, and the special votes counted over the two weeks after election night.

What goes in
1
How many votes are left

The Electoral Commission does not publish an electorate's final total until every vote has been counted, so it has to be estimated. Enrolment in the electorate, and the turnout there last time, give the likely size of the vote; subtract what is already counted and the rest is still to come.

Both figures are only a guide. A roll that has grown, or unusual turnout, can make the estimate wrong in either direction, so the estimate is checked against the booths. If most of an electorate's booths are still to report, the electorate is far from finished.

2
Where the rest of them are heading

The starting point is this electorate's own result last time, the only complete picture of how the electorate votes. Tonight's results then move it: in the electorates counted so far the vote has swung five points to Labour, and that swing is applied to this electorate's last result. What comes out is the shape the remaining votes are expected to take.

Early in the evening we lean on tonight's swing less: the first votes counted are advance votes, and the booths that report first are not a random sample of the electorate. Neither is a reliable guide to how the electorate's election-day votes will split.

How those become one number
1
The expected result

Adding the expected remaining votes to the votes already counted gives one expected result. Here that is a National lead of about 2: National won by 12 last time, and a five-point swing to Labour takes 10 points off a lead between two parties.

2
How far it could still move

The model keeps every result that is still plausible, weighted by how likely it is. That collection is the spread. It is wide when a lot is still to count, when the race is close, and when last time's result is a weak guide to the electorate. It narrows as the votes come in.

3
Turning the spread into Win %

Win % is the share of the spread in which a candidate finishes ahead. Here 54% of it is on National's side of a tie and 46% on Labour's, so National's Win % is 54.

4
Adding the special votes

About one vote in five is a special vote: one cast from overseas, or one cast outside the voter's own electorate. They are counted after election night and added to each candidate's total from that night, so they change the lead only if they split differently from the votes counted on election night. The lead here is 393 votes against about 9,700 special votes, so the special vote would have to favour Labour by four points more than the election-night count did to overturn it.

The model lets the special vote's lean run either way, with a ten-point spread: about three times the swing a typical seat showed in the last three elections. Half of that spread leans towards the leader, which keeps the electorate; in the other half, only shifts of more than four points overturn the lead. That leaves the lead surviving in 66 cases out of 100.

Once the ordinary count is finished, every ordinary vote is in but the special votes can still change the winner. From that point Win % is this figure: the chance the lead survives the special votes.

The red tail is the 34 cases in 100 where the special votes shift far enough to overturn the lead.

The order is the model's, because the question is. A reader arriving here already has the number; what they do not have is what it is made of. So the two inputs come first, then the four steps that turn them into one figure, and the figure itself arrives at step 3, where it can be pointed at rather than merely stated.

Every diagram is a share of one line. The counted bar, the party bars, the swing arrow, the two ranges and the special-vote scale are the same drawing at different scales (a track, a fill, a hairline rule), which is what lets eight figures sit on one page without becoming a report. The curve is the only new shape, and it is there because a range is not a thing a bar can show.

The wide/narrow pair is the load-bearing drawing. It says the one thing a single number cannot: the same electorate, the same lead, and a Win % that moves because the amount counted moved. Without it a reader reasonably assumes the percentage is a fixed property of the votes rather than a statement about what is still out.

What each step adds Step 1 is arithmetic a reader can check (12 − 10 = 2). Step 2 is why the answer is a range rather than a point, and why the range narrows. Step 3 turns the range into a percentage, written as a share of the spread. Step 4 is the special votes, which change the lead, the same quantity the whole page is about, and so are priced against it.

Copy notes
two rules the page follows, and the numbers it pins
No notationThe page carries no formula and no symbols. Its one quotient (393 ÷ 9,700 ≈ 4) is written out, because it is the only step a reader is likely to want to redo.
Not simulatedThe model computes the spread rather than sampling it, so the page never says “we ran it 10,000 times”. It says what the figure is, not a method it does not use.
The special vote's leanMeasured, not assumed: scripts/measure-specials-swing.mjs derives each seat's special-vote split by subtraction (the per-voting-place pages list overseas, advance and election-day specials per candidate above the seat total), giving a within-cycle sd of 4.75 points across 213 seats and cycle means of +2.3, +1.5 and −3.3. The page's ten points is deliberately about twice the measured spread, which is why it can say the model allows more room than recent elections needed.
One word per ideaLead, spread, tie and Win %: each is defined once and then used unchanged. The special votes are priced against the lead, not against “the margin”, because the page has already called it the lead.
Fixed numbers54, 46, 393, 9,700, 4, 10 and 66 are illustrative but self-consistent, so a test can pin them; none of them is read from live data.

The date check on eligibility. “Enrolled late” was the wrong reason for a special vote after the Electoral Amendment Act 2025: enrolment now closes 13 days before election day (25 October 2026) and nobody can enrol at a voting place. The page therefore names only the two reasons that still hold (overseas votes and votes cast outside the voter's electorate) and does not offer the list as exhaustive.

Why the tie line matters. The word “level” reads as “level pegging” to some and “a level” to others. Tie is the one word that cannot be misread, and it is the same word the reader would use.

What the model's own vocabulary is allowed to do. Swing, special votes and probability stay, because they are precise and understood. The words that were invented to avoid them (“endings”, “one guess at the finish”) were the ambiguity, and are gone.

Costs and aftercare

One hidden route/how-win-percent-works. No nav entry, no change to Layout, no modal state to manage. Because it is a page, it survives a refresh, a share and a bookmark. A dialog would not.
The copy is the cost≈590 words of prose. Every paragraph describes the model, so a change to turnout, swing or special votes means reading them again, for the same reason docs/seat-probability-model.md exists.
A test that pins the claimThe page must print 54 and 46 as complementary, and 66 for the special-vote step. The numbers are fixed, not live, so a snapshot on the text is enough.
AccessibilityA real link with a real aria-label on the ⓘ; one h1; every diagram has an aria-label saying what it shows, and no meaning is carried by colour alone: the party bars are also labelled, and the range is described in the sentence beside it.
